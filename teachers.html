The Multiplier

One Teacher, Many Futures

A student who competes changes their own life. A teacher who champions competition changes hundreds.

Behind almost every student who discovers what they are capable of, there is a teacher who saw it first and refused to let it go unnoticed. That teacher could be you.

1

There is a particular kind of influence that only teachers have. A parent shapes one child; a teacher shapes cohort after cohort, year after year, each carrying forward whatever the teacher managed to awaken in them. When a teacher decides that their students should not merely learn the syllabus but test themselves against the wider world, that single decision ripples outward across hundreds of young lives. The student who would never have entered a competition alone enters because a trusted teacher believed they should — and that entry can be the beginning of everything that follows.

2

This is why we think of teachers not as users of the platform but as its most important partners. The competitions, the criteria, the certificates — all of it is infrastructure. What actually turns a hesitant student into a competitor is a human being who knows them, sees their potential, and gives them the nudge and the belief to try. No platform can replace that, and we do not pretend to. What we can do is give teachers a powerful, ready-made tool for stretching their students, and make it as easy as possible to use.

3

This page is written for educators: the teachers wondering whether competitions are worth the effort, how to fit them around an already-full workload, and what role they might play. It makes the case that competitions are among the most effective teaching tools available — and that a teacher’s involvement, however modest, can be the decisive factor in a young person’s growth. Whether you have five minutes to mention an opportunity or the enthusiasm to mentor a whole class through it, there is a place for you here.

A Teaching Tool

Competitions in the Classroom

Beyond the results, competitions are one of the most versatile instruments in a teacher’s kit.

Stretch Your Best

Competitions give your most capable students somewhere to reach when the syllabus no longer challenges them — enrichment you do not have to design from scratch.

Reach the Overlooked

A student who is unremarkable in exams may be brilliant in a design or writing challenge. Competitions let you find and nurture talent that conventional assessment misses entirely.

Deepen Learning

Preparing for a competition forces students to apply knowledge rather than memorise it, producing the deep, durable understanding that ordinary revision rarely achieves.

Motivate a Class

A shared goal and a little friendly rivalry can energise a whole classroom, turning routine into purpose and pulling reluctant students along with the momentum.

Teach Real Skills

Competitions build time management, resilience, and the ability to perform under pressure — the capacities you know matter but the curriculum rarely lets you teach directly.

Give Tangible Proof

Your students earn verifiable certificates that reward their effort and reflect well on your teaching — concrete evidence of the growth you helped create.

Your Role

Ways to Contribute

There is a level of involvement to suit every teacher, from a quick mention to deep mentorship.

1

One of the most reassuring things about supporting competitions is that you get to choose how involved you are, and even the lightest touch makes a genuine difference. At the simplest level, you can be a signpost: mentioning an upcoming competition to the students you think would benefit, and encouraging them to enter. This costs almost nothing and yet it is often the single decisive act — because most students who would thrive in competition simply never hear about it, or never believe it is for them until a teacher says otherwise.

2

With a little more time, you can be a mentor: helping students understand the brief, pointing them toward the right preparation, offering feedback on their approach (though never doing the work for them), and steadying their nerves before the day. Mentorship does not require you to be an expert in the competition’s subject; it requires you to be the experienced, encouraging presence that helps a young person prepare well and compete with confidence. Many teachers find this the most rewarding part of the whole endeavour.

3

If you have the enthusiasm, you can go further still — becoming a coordinator who brings competitions to your whole school, or contributing your subject expertise as a reviewer or content contributor as the platform grows. Educators who believe in fair, merit-based assessment are exactly the people we want shaping and judging competitions, and there are meaningful ways to contribute described on our Careers & Volunteering page. But let us be clear: none of this is required. A teacher who does nothing more than encourage the right student to enter has already done something valuable.

No Extra Burden

Fitting It Around a Full Workload

The honest concern of every teacher — addressed directly.

1

Every teacher reading this is already stretched, and the last thing any of you needs is another demand on your time. So let us be honest about what supporting competitions actually requires: far less than you might fear. Because competitions run online and around your existing schedule, they do not need to occupy classroom time or generate marking. The platform handles registration, judging, and certification; the published resources handle much of the preparation guidance. What is left for you is the human part — encouragement, a little mentorship, a moment of celebration — which is precisely the part teachers do best and machines cannot do at all.

2

In fact, competitions can lighten your load in some respects rather than adding to it. A well-chosen competition is a ready-made enrichment activity for your most capable students — challenge you would otherwise have to design yourself. It is a source of motivation that makes students want to engage with your subject more deeply. And it is a way to reach students who have quietly disengaged from conventional lessons, offering them a different arena in which to rediscover that they are capable. Used well, competition is not one more thing on your plate; it is a tool that makes several of the things already on your plate easier.

3

Our advice to busy teachers is to start small and let it grow naturally. Pick one competition that suits a few of your students, encourage them to enter, and support them lightly through it. See how it feels, and how they respond. Almost invariably, the experience of watching a student rise to a challenge — and knowing you helped make it happen — is energising rather than draining. From that first small step, deeper involvement tends to follow because you want it to, not because anyone asked.

The Gift You Give

Spotting the Talent Others Miss

The most valuable thing a teacher can do is see a student before they see themselves.

1

Teachers possess something no algorithm and no exam can replicate: the ability to notice a spark in a particular student that the student has not yet noticed in themselves. You see the quiet child whose essays are quietly extraordinary, the restless student who comes alive when a problem is genuinely hard, the daydreamer whose ideas are more original than anyone realises. Conventional assessment routinely overlooks these students, because it measures a narrow band of ability under narrow conditions. You see the wider picture, and competitions give you somewhere to send the talent you spot.

2

Matching a student to the right competition is one of the most powerful interventions a teacher can make. The child who struggles with timed exams may flourish in a submission-based design challenge, where care and creativity matter more than speed. The student who is bored by routine may be transformed by a problem-solving contest that finally stretches them. By knowing your students and knowing the range of competitions available — across STEM, literary, creative, design, quizzing, and entrepreneurship — you can place each young person in the arena where their particular strength can finally show. For that student, being seen and directed in this way can change how they see their own future.

3

This is, perhaps, the deepest reason to get involved. Every teacher can name a student whose potential went unrecognised, and every teacher knows the quiet ache of that missed chance. Competitions are a way to make sure fewer such students slip through — to give the overlooked child a stage, and the doubting child a reason to believe. When you encourage the right student to enter and watch them discover what they can do, you are doing the very thing that drew most teachers to the profession in the first place: helping a young person become more than they thought they could be.

For You, Too

Growth & Recognition for Educators

Supporting competitions gives back to the teacher, not only the student.

1

It would be incomplete to describe only what competitions do for students, because they give something to teachers too. Mentoring students through competition sharpens your own understanding of your subject and of how young people learn under pressure. It connects you with a community of educators across India who share your commitment to stretching students beyond the syllabus. And it offers a form of professional satisfaction that routine teaching, for all its rewards, does not always provide: the vivid, unambiguous experience of watching a student you believed in succeed on a stage far larger than your classroom.

2

There is recognition in it as well. A teacher whose students consistently rise to challenges and earn genuine achievement is demonstrating something real about the quality of their teaching — evidence that stands independently of any internal assessment. As the platform grows, we intend to celebrate the educators and schools whose students excel, because the teachers behind student success deserve to be seen just as much as the students themselves. The certificate a student earns is, in a quiet way, a certificate for the teacher who helped make it possible.

3

Most of all, involvement in competitions reconnects many teachers with why they started. Amid the marking, the administration, and the pressures of the modern classroom, it is easy to lose sight of the simple joy of igniting a young mind. Competitions bring that joy back into focus. They give you moments — a student’s disbelief at their own result, a shy child’s first taste of recognition, a whole class buzzing with purpose — that remind you exactly why this work matters. That renewal is not a small thing, and it is one of the quieter gifts of getting involved.

Teacher Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions educators ask most about supporting competitions.

I’m already overloaded. How much time does this really take?

As little as you want. At the lightest level, simply mentioning a competition to the right students takes minutes and is often the decisive act. The platform handles registration, judging, and certificates; the resources handle much of the preparation. What’s left for you is encouragement — the human part teachers do best.

Do I need to be an expert in the competition’s subject to help?

No. Mentorship is about helping students understand the brief, prepare well, and compete with confidence — not about being a subject expert. Your value is your experience with young people and your ability to encourage and steady them, which applies across every category.

Can I help students with their actual entries?

You can guide, question, and encourage — but the work itself must be the student’s own, both because the rules on originality require it and because doing it for them robs them of the confidence competition is meant to build. Be a sounding board, not a co-author.

How do I choose which students to encourage?

Look beyond exam results. The quiet child whose writing is extraordinary, the restless student who comes alive at hard problems, the creative daydreamer — competitions across many categories let you match each student to the arena where their particular strength can show. Trust what you see in them.

What’s the easiest way to get started?

Start small: pick one competition that suits a few of your students, encourage them to enter, and support them lightly. See how they respond. Deeper involvement tends to follow naturally once you’ve watched a student rise to a challenge you helped set in motion.

Can I bring competitions to my whole school?

Yes. Enthusiastic teachers often make the best coordinators. Our Schools programme explains how to bring competitions to your whole institution, and how the coordinator role works in practice.

Are there ways to contribute my expertise more deeply?

Yes. As the platform grows, educators who believe in fair, merit-based assessment can contribute as reviewers or content contributors. Our Careers & Volunteering page is where those conversations begin.

How do competitions benefit me as a teacher?

They sharpen your subject understanding, connect you with a community of educators, and offer the deep satisfaction of watching students you believed in succeed. They also provide independent evidence of the quality of your teaching — and often reconnect teachers with why they started.

In Practice

Weaving Competition Into Your Teaching

A few practical ways to make competition a natural extension of what you already do.

1

Competition works best when it is woven into the ordinary fabric of your teaching rather than bolted on as a separate chore. One simple approach is to align a competition with a topic you are already teaching, so preparation reinforces the curriculum instead of competing with it — a science competition alongside a relevant unit, a writing challenge during a literature module. Students then see their classroom learning come alive with real purpose, and you get the motivational lift of a genuine goal without inventing extra content. The competition becomes an application of what they were learning anyway.

2

Another practical move is to use competition as a source of aspiration for the whole class, even if only a few enter. Sharing what past participants achieved, celebrating the students who take part, and treating competition as a normal, admired part of classroom life gradually shifts the culture. Students who watch peers attempt challenges become more willing to attempt their own, and the quiet message — that ambition is welcome here — reaches even those who never formally compete. A single visible participant can inspire several future ones.

3

Finally, make celebration a habit. When a student earns a certificate or a strong result, mark it — a word in class, a mention to the year group, a note home to parents. Recognition costs nothing and teaches everyone watching that effort and courage are valued. Over time these small acts compound into a classroom, and eventually a school, where testing yourself is simply what students do. You do not need a grand programme to build that culture; you need only the consistent, human habit of noticing and celebrating the students who dare to try.

Quick answers to the questions students, parents, and teachers ask most.

How do teachers support students in competitions?

Teachers guide preparation, share published criteria, and encourage participation. Our resources and the judging process page make it easy to set clear expectations.

Do teachers need special training to take part?

No special training is required. The process is designed to be simple and transparent — the how competitions work guide covers everything from registration to recognition.

How can teachers bring competitions to a whole class or school?

Teachers can coordinate group participation and work with their school through our Schools programme. Get in touch via the contact page and we'll help you set it up.

How do students receive recognition?

Every participant earns a verifiable certificate, and winners receive medals and awards — all confirmable through certificate verification.
