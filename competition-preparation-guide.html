How to Prepare for a Student Competition

Great results rarely come from talent alone — they come from smart, steady preparation. This practical guide walks students, parents, and teachers through a simple, proven approach to getting competition-ready with confidence.

Preparation Is a Skill — Here’s How to Master It

Talent gets you to the start line. Preparation is what carries you across the finish.

Most competitions are not won on the day. They are won in the quiet hours of preparation that came before — and preparing well is a skill anyone can learn.

1

There is a comforting myth that the students who win competitions are simply more gifted than everyone else, and that the rest of us are spectators to their talent. It is mostly false. Behind almost every strong performance is not a mysterious gift but a set of deliberate, learnable habits: reading the brief properly, decoding what the judges actually reward, planning the work across time, practising under realistic conditions, and polishing relentlessly before submitting. These are not talents you are born with. They are techniques, and this guide will teach them to you.

2

What follows is a complete, practical preparation system, organised as phases you can move through for any competition in any category. It is deliberately specific — you will find actual methods, not vague encouragement — because the difference between a good result and a disappointing one usually comes down to specifics: the instruction you did or did not read carefully, the extra draft you did or did not write, the day of rest you did or did not build into your plan. Master the specifics, and you remove the avoidable mistakes that quietly sink far more capable students than a genuine lack of ability ever does.

3

Read the phases in order for a full system, or jump to the part you need right now. Whether this is your first competition or your fifteenth, treat preparation as the real competition — the one you have the most control over — and you will walk into the actual event calmer, sharper, and ready to do yourself justice.

Phase One

Decode the Brief & the Rubric

Before you prepare anything, understand exactly what you are being asked to do — and how it will be judged.

1

The single most common cause of a disappointing result is not a lack of ability; it is a misread brief. A student pours effort into an excellent piece of work that answers a slightly different question than the one that was asked, and loses marks they never needed to lose. So the very first act of preparation, before any studying or creating, is to read the brief slowly, more than once, and make sure you understand every word of it. What exactly is being asked? What are the constraints — the word limit, the format, the theme, the deadline? What is explicitly required, and what is merely optional? Write these down in your own words. If anything is unclear, re-read the competition page, because it is the final authority.

2

Then turn to the rubric — the published breakdown of how your work will be scored — and treat it as your map. The rubric tells you precisely what the judges reward and how much each element is worth. If originality carries thirty per cent and presentation carries five, you now know where to spend your energy and where a little is enough. Preparing against the rubric is not gaming the system; it is simply understanding what excellence looks like for this particular challenge. Print it out, keep it beside you throughout, and check your work against it at every stage.

3

This phase takes half an hour and saves you from the most expensive mistakes there are. A student who deeply understands the brief and the rubric before they begin is already ahead of most of the field — not because they are cleverer, but because they know exactly what they are aiming at while others are still guessing.

  • Read the brief at least twice, slowly, and restate it in your own words
  • List every constraint: word limit, format, theme, deadline, requirements
  • Study the rubric and note the weight of each criterion
  • Identify where the marks are — and where they are not
  • Resolve anything unclear against the competition page before you start
Phase Two

Plan Backwards From the Deadline

The students who never panic are simply the ones who planned not to.

1

Once you understand the task, resist the urge to dive straight into the work. Spend a little time planning instead, because a plan is what converts a distant deadline from a source of dread into a series of manageable steps. The most reliable method is to plan backwards. Start from the deadline and work in reverse: leave a buffer of at least a day before the deadline for final review and submission, then a block for polishing, then a block for producing the work, then a block for building the knowledge or skills you need, and finally the time between now and then in which all of this must fit. Suddenly a vague ‘two weeks’ becomes a concrete schedule.

2

Building in a buffer is not optional; it is the difference between calm and chaos. Something will go wrong — a busy day at school, a technical hiccup, an idea that needs reworking — and the buffer is what absorbs it. Students who plan to finish exactly at the deadline finish late or submit in a panic. Students who plan to finish a full day early almost always finish on time, and submit work they have actually had a chance to review with fresh eyes. Aim to be done before you have to be done.

3

Finally, be honest about the time you actually have. Look at your real week — school, other commitments, rest — and schedule preparation into the gaps that genuinely exist, not the ones you wish existed. A realistic plan you can keep beats an ambitious one you will abandon. Break the work into small, specific sessions with a clear goal for each, so that every time you sit down you know exactly what you are doing. Momentum comes from finishing small things, not from staring at big ones.

Phase Three

Build the Knowledge & Skill

Now do the actual work of getting better — the right way, not just the hard way.

1

This is the phase most people think of as ‘preparation’: the studying, the practising, the building of whatever knowledge or skill the competition demands. How you spend this time matters enormously, because effort applied badly produces far less than effort applied well. The golden rule is to practise actively, not passively. Re-reading notes and watching explanations feels productive but builds only a shallow, fragile familiarity. What builds durable ability is retrieval and application: testing yourself, solving problems, producing work, and getting feedback — struggling a little, because the struggle is where the learning happens.

2

Practise under conditions as close as possible to the real thing. If the competition is a timed quiz, do timed practice, not leisurely review, so that you train your composure and pacing along with your knowledge. If it is a submission, produce full practice pieces rather than fragments, so you experience the whole process of making something complete. This kind of realistic rehearsal does something ordinary study cannot: it prepares you not just to know the material but to perform with it under the specific pressures of the actual event. Our Resources are built to support exactly this kind of active, targeted practice.

3

Be strategic about where you focus. The rubric already told you where the marks are; your practice should follow the marks. It is tempting to spend time on the parts you already enjoy and are good at, but the biggest gains almost always come from honestly identifying your weakest area and deliberately working on it. Growth lives at the edge of your comfort, not in the middle of it. A student who spends their preparation strengthening their weaknesses, rather than polishing their strengths, arrives at the competition far more complete.

Phase Four

Produce, Then Iterate

For submission-based competitions, the first version is never the one that wins.

1

If your competition involves producing something — an essay, a design, a project, an invention — the most important thing to understand is that your first attempt is a draft, not a final answer, and it is meant to be. Almost no strong piece of work springs out fully formed. It emerges through iteration: a rough version made real, then examined honestly, then improved, then improved again. The students who produce excellent work are rarely the ones who got it right first time; they are the ones who were willing to make it, look at it clearly, and make it better.

2

So get a complete draft done early, even a rough one, because a finished rough draft is infinitely more useful than a perfect fragment. Once the whole thing exists, you can see its shape, find its weak points, and strengthen them. Then set it aside — ideally for a day or two — before you return to revise it. Distance is a superpower in editing. Work you were too close to see clearly reveals its flaws and its opportunities the moment you come back to it with fresh eyes. This is why the backward plan built in time before the deadline: that time is what makes iteration possible.

3

As you revise, be willing to cut. The instinct of most students is to add — more words, more features, more everything — but strong work is usually the result of subtraction. Remove what does not serve the core idea. Sharpen what remains. Check every revision against the rubric to make sure you are strengthening exactly what the judges reward. A piece that says less, but says it better and more clearly, almost always outperforms one that says more but loses its focus in the process.

Phase Five

Review, Polish & Submit

The final ten per cent of care is where good work becomes winning work.

1

With your work substantially done and your buffer day arrived, the task shifts from creating to refining. This final phase is where careless students throw away marks they had already earned, and where careful students quietly pull ahead. Go back to the brief and the rubric one last time and check, point by point, that you have done everything asked and answered exactly what was set. It is heartbreakingly common to produce excellent work that overlooks a stated requirement — a word limit exceeded, a format not followed, an element of the brief simply forgotten. Catch these now, while you still can.

2

Then polish the details. Read written work aloud to catch the awkward sentences your eye glides over. Check a design at full size and in the format it will be judged in. Proofread for the small errors — the typo, the mislabelled file, the missing detail — that signal carelessness and cost easy presentation marks. None of this is glamorous, but the difference between work that feels finished and work that feels rushed is almost entirely in this final layer of care. Judges notice it, even when they cannot name it, and the rubric usually rewards it directly.

3

Finally, submit with room to spare. Confirm the exact format required and match it precisely. Upload well before the deadline, not at it, so that a slow connection or a technical glitch cannot undo weeks of work in the final minutes. Once submitted, check your confirmation and then — genuinely — let it go. You have done the work; the result is now out of your hands, and worrying about it changes nothing. The discipline of submitting early and then releasing the outcome is itself a mark of a seasoned competitor.

A simple rule that prevents the most painful losses: aim to submit a full day before the deadline. Treat the real deadline as if it were twenty-four hours earlier, and the genuine deadline becomes a safety net you never actually need.

Tailored Tips

Advice by Category

Different kinds of competition reward different kinds of preparation.

Quizzes & Tests

Practise under strict time limits to train pacing and composure. Learn to move on from a hard question rather than sinking time into it. Breadth beats depth — a wide base of solid knowledge usually outscores a narrow, deep one.

Writing

Plan your structure before you write a sentence; a clear skeleton is what the ‘structure’ criterion rewards. Draft, rest, then edit ruthlessly. Read the final piece aloud — your ear catches what your eye misses.

Design & Creative

Start with the idea, not the software. Sketch and explore concepts on paper first, then execute the strongest one cleanly. Originality is usually weighted heavily — resist the first, most obvious idea and push for a fresher one.

STEM & Problem-Solving

Master the fundamentals until they are automatic, so your mind is free for the hard part. Practise unfamiliar problems, not just familiar ones. Show your reasoning clearly — method often earns marks even when the final answer slips.

Entrepreneurship

Solve a real problem for a real person, and be able to explain who and why. Judges reward clarity and feasibility over grand but vague ambition. Anticipate the obvious objection and answer it before it is asked.

Multi-Round

Pace yourself across the whole competition, not just the first round. Treat an early screening seriously enough to advance, but keep energy in reserve for the deeper challenge that consistency, not a single burst, will decide.

Avoid These

The Mistakes That Sink Good Students

Most disappointing results come not from weakness but from these avoidable errors.

1

It is worth studying failure directly, because the same handful of mistakes account for the great majority of disappointing results — and every one of them is avoidable. The first and most common is not reading the brief carefully, and answering a slightly different question than the one asked. The second is leaving everything to the last minute, which removes any chance to iterate, review, or absorb the inevitable setback. The third is ignoring the rubric and pouring effort into elements the judges barely reward while neglecting the ones that carry the most marks.

2

Others are subtler but just as costly. Never testing under realistic conditions leaves a well-prepared student unravelled by the pressure of the real thing. Adding instead of editing produces bloated work that buries a good idea under unnecessary weight. Polishing strengths while ignoring weaknesses caps a student’s score exactly where they are weakest. And submitting at the deadline rather than before it hands the outcome to chance, letting a technical glitch or a slow connection undo weeks of genuine effort in the final minutes.

3

Notice that not one of these mistakes is about ability. They are all about process — about how a student approaches the task rather than how talented they are. That is genuinely good news, because process is entirely within your control. A student of ordinary ability with an excellent process routinely outperforms a more gifted one with a careless approach. Read this list before every competition, and simply refusing to make these mistakes will place you ahead of much of the field before you have written a single word.

Steady Nerves

Managing the Pressure

A calm competitor performs closer to their true ability than an anxious one.

1

All the preparation in the world can be undermined by nerves on the day, so managing pressure is itself a part of preparing well. The good news is that thorough preparation is, by far, the best cure for anxiety: it is very hard to feel panicked about a challenge you have genuinely rehearsed. Much of what feels like nerves is really the discomfort of uncertainty, and preparation replaces uncertainty with familiarity. When you have practised under realistic conditions, the real event feels less like a threat and more like something you have done before — because, in a sense, you have.

2

In the final days, protect the basics that anxiety loves to attack: sleep, food, and rest. A tired brain performs worse than a well-rested one, no matter how much it studied, so resist the temptation to cram through the night before — the marginal knowledge you gain is dwarfed by the clarity you lose. On the day itself, arrive early, breathe slowly, and remind yourself that you have prepared and that this is simply a chance to show what you already know. A few slow breaths genuinely lower the body’s stress response and steady the mind.

3

Above all, keep the result in perspective. One competition, at one moment, does not define your ability or your worth, and reminding yourself of that paradoxically helps you perform better — because the fear of a bad outcome is what tightens the mind and clouds the thinking. Compete to do your best and to learn, rather than to avoid disaster, and you free yourself to perform closer to your true ability. The students who handle pressure best are not the ones who care least; they are the ones who have put the stakes in their proper place.

Set the Stage

Your Environment & Tools

Where and how you prepare quietly shapes how well you prepare.

1

Preparation does not happen in a vacuum; it happens in a place, at a time, surrounded by whatever you have allowed into the room. Getting these conditions right removes a surprising amount of friction. Find a spot where you can focus without interruption, even if it is only a corner of a table for an hour at a time. Put the phone out of reach rather than merely face-down, because the mere presence of a notification-capable device measurably fragments attention. The goal is not a perfect study; it is a space where your mind can settle into the work without a fight.

2

Gather your tools before you begin a session, not during it. Keep the brief and the printed rubric beside you at all times — they are your compass, and glancing at them constantly keeps you honest about what you are aiming for. Have your practice materials, your drafts, and whatever the task requires within reach, so that starting is effortless and you never lose momentum hunting for something. A little setup at the start of each session pays for itself many times over in focus.

3

Pay attention, too, to when you work best. Most people have a window in the day when their mind is naturally sharpest — for many students it is the morning, for others the evening — and it is worth protecting that window for your hardest preparation, leaving lighter review for your lower-energy times. Working with your natural rhythm rather than against it means the same hour of effort produces noticeably more. Small, consistent sessions in your best window will carry you further than occasional marathons in your worst.

The Long Game

From One Competition to a Practice

The real prize is the preparation habit you carry into everything that follows.

1

Everything in this guide will help you prepare for a single competition, but its deeper value appears when you treat preparation not as a one-time scramble but as a skill you refine across many competitions. The first time you work through these phases, they will feel deliberate and slightly effortful, like learning any new method. By the third or fourth time, they begin to feel natural. By the tenth, they have become simply how you approach any challenge with a deadline attached — which is to say, they have become a life skill disguised as a competition tip.

2

This is why it is worth keeping a light record of what worked and what did not after each competition. A few honest notes — where you spent your time, what you would do differently, which mistakes you made — turn every competition into a lesson that improves the next one. Students who reflect in this way improve remarkably quickly, not because they are more talented but because they are compounding their experience rather than repeating it. Each competition makes them better at competing, and the gains stack.

3

Seen this way, preparation stops being a chore you endure before an event and becomes a craft you are steadily mastering — one that will serve you in examinations, in university, and in any work that ever asks you to deliver something good by a certain date. The certificate you earn from any single competition is real and worth having. But the preparation habit you build across many of them is the thing that will quietly shape your whole future, long after the specifics of any one contest are forgotten.

Common Questions

Preparation, Answered

The practical questions students ask most about getting ready to compete.

How far in advance should I start preparing?

As soon as you register. Registering early gives you the longest window and lets you plan across days rather than cramming. Even a modest amount of preparation, spread out and done well, beats a frantic effort at the last minute.

I don’t know where to begin. What’s the first step?

Read the brief and the rubric closely, and restate the task in your own words. Understanding exactly what is being asked, and how it will be judged, is the foundation everything else builds on. Only once that is clear should you start studying or creating.

How do I prepare if I’m not sure what to study?

Let the rubric guide you — it tells you precisely what the judges reward. Focus your practice on the highest-weighted criteria and, honestly, on your weakest areas within them. Our Resources offer category-specific material to work from.

Is it better to practise a lot or to study the theory?

Active practice beats passive study almost every time. Testing yourself, solving problems, and producing full practice pieces builds durable, usable ability; re-reading notes builds only shallow familiarity. Practise under conditions close to the real event.

How many drafts should I do for a submission?

As many as it takes, but at least a rough complete draft followed by one substantial revision with fresh eyes. The first version is never the strongest — the magic is in returning to it after a short break and improving it. Build time for this into your plan.

What’s the most common preparation mistake?

Misreading the brief and answering a slightly different question than the one asked. It is entirely avoidable: read the brief at least twice, list every requirement, and check your finished work against it point by point.

How do I handle nerves on the day?

Prepare thoroughly — it is the best cure for anxiety — then protect your sleep and rest in the final days. On the day, arrive early, breathe slowly, and keep the result in perspective. Competing to do your best, rather than to avoid disaster, frees you to perform better.

Should I aim to submit right at the deadline?

No — aim to submit a full day early. Treating the real deadline as if it were twenty-four hours earlier gives you a safety net against technical glitches and slow connections, and means weeks of work are never undone in the final minutes.
