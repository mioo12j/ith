For Parents & Guardians

A Parent’s Guide

Practical, reassuring guidance to help your child get the most out of competing — with confidence and without pressure.

For Parents

Your Role Is Bigger Than You Think

You will not sit the competition. But whether your child thrives in it often comes down to you.

This guide is written for you — the parent or guardian standing beside a child who is about to test themselves. What you say, and how you say it, matters more than any tip we could give the student.

1

When a child enters a competition, the visible work belongs to them: the preparation, the effort, the submission. But there is an invisible layer underneath, and that layer belongs to you. It is the atmosphere at home in the week before the deadline. It is the tone of your voice when you ask how it is going. It is what happens at the dinner table on the day the result arrives. Children read these signals long before they read any rubric, and they shape whether competition becomes a source of growth or a source of fear.

2

The good news is that supporting your child well does not require you to understand the subject, coach the content, or have competed yourself. It requires something simpler and harder: the discipline to encourage without pressuring, to be interested without taking over, and to make it unmistakably clear that your love and pride do not depend on where they finish. This guide will show you, concretely, how to do that — and how the platform itself is built to make your job easier by keeping everything safe, fair, and transparent.

3

Read it in full if you can. It is longer than a checklist because parenting a young competitor is not a checklist — it is a series of small, human moments where the right instinct is not always the obvious one. Our aim is to help you get those moments right.

Why It Matters

What Your Child Actually Gains

The certificate is the smallest part of what a competition gives a young person.

Confidence That Is Earned

Real confidence does not come from praise; it comes from doing something difficult and surviving the outcome. A competition gives your child a genuine challenge and the chance to discover they can rise to it.

The Habit of Effort

Preparing across days or weeks teaches a child to set a goal and work towards it steadily — a skill that transfers directly into exams, and eventually into work and life.

Resilience

Learning to submit something you cannot take back, and to accept the result with grace, builds an emotional strength that protects children far beyond any single event.

Perspective

Competing alongside students across India helps a child see where they stand honestly, celebrate others, and understand that there is always more to learn.

A Credible Record

A verifiable certificate is something concrete your child can point to — on a school portfolio, a scholarship form, or an application — that says they tried and they followed through.

Self-Knowledge

Competitions help a child discover what genuinely excites them. Sometimes the most valuable result is simply realising, "I love this," or "this isn’t for me — but that one is."

The Golden Rule

Encourage, Don’t Pressure

The single most important thing you can get right — and the easiest to get wrong.

1

There is a fine but crucial line between encouragement and pressure, and children feel exactly which side you are on. Encouragement says: I believe you can do hard things, and I am here whatever happens. Pressure says: my approval depends on your performance. The words can sound almost identical — ‘I know you’ll do brilliantly’ can be either, depending on what your child hears underneath it. The difference is whether your child feels supported to try, or terrified to fail.

2

Pressure is rarely intended. It usually comes from love — a parent who wants the best for their child and lets that hope harden into expectation. But its effects are real: a pressured child prepares anxiously, competes fearfully, and experiences any result short of winning as a personal failure and a disappointment to you. Over time, they may stop trying difficult things altogether, because the risk of letting you down outweighs the reward of growth. That is the opposite of what competition should do.

3

The antidote is to praise the process, not just the outcome. Notice the effort, the planning, the courage it took to enter at all. Ask about what they found interesting, not only what they scored. And say the words that matter most, out loud, before the result is known: that you are proud of them for trying, and that nothing about the result will change how you feel. A child who competes from that foundation is free to do their best — which, not coincidentally, is also when they perform best.

A simple test: after a conversation about the competition, does your child seem more relaxed and motivated, or more tense and worried? The first is encouragement working. The second is a signal to soften your approach, however well-intentioned it was.

Practical Support

How to Help — Without Taking Over

Your job is to build the runway, not to fly the plane.

1

The most useful support you can offer is practical and quietly invisible. Help your child protect a little time and space to prepare, free from interruptions and screens they do not need. Help them plan backwards from the deadline, so the work is spread across days rather than crammed into a panicked final night. Make sure they have eaten and slept before a timed round, because no amount of preparation survives an exhausted mind. These small logistics are where a parent genuinely moves the needle.

2

What you should resist — however tempting — is doing the work itself. The moment a parent starts writing the essay, correcting the design, or supplying the answers, the achievement stops being the child’s. Worse, it teaches the child that they could not have done it alone, which erodes the very confidence competition is meant to build. It also breaches the rules on originality that protect every honest participant. Your child’s imperfect, genuine work is worth infinitely more than your polished contribution.

3

By all means be a sounding board. Ask questions that help them think — ‘what are you most proud of in this?’, ‘is there anything the instructions asked for that you haven’t covered?’ — rather than handing them answers. The goal is a child who finishes able to say, honestly, ‘I did this,’ with you as the reason they had the calm, the time, and the belief to do it well.

  • Do help protect quiet, focused time to prepare
  • Do help plan the work across days, not one late night
  • Do ask questions that prompt their own thinking
  • Don’t write, correct, or complete the work yourself
  • Don’t hover or check in so often that it adds pressure
  • Don’t compare your child to a sibling, classmate, or their past self
The Words

What to Say — and When

Sometimes the right sentence at the right moment is the whole job.

1

Parents often ask what they should actually say. Here are the moments that matter most, and words that tend to help. When they register: ‘I’m glad you’re giving this a go — that takes courage.’ This frames entering itself as the brave, praiseworthy act it is, before performance even enters the picture.

2

While they prepare: ‘How’s it going? What’s been the most interesting part?’ Notice this asks about their experience, not their likelihood of winning. It signals that you care about them, not just the result. The night before: ‘You’ve prepared well. Whatever happens tomorrow, I’m proud of you for trying. Now go and sleep.’ This removes the fear of disappointing you at exactly the moment that fear does the most damage.

3

When they win: ‘You earned that — through the work you put in. Well done.’ Tie the success to effort, so they learn that results follow work rather than luck or identity. When they don’t: ‘I know that’s disappointing. I’m still proud of you, and I mean that. What did you learn that you’ll use next time?’ This validates the feeling, reaffirms your love unconditionally, and gently turns the moment towards growth. Avoid, at all costs, ‘it doesn’t matter’ — it clearly mattered to them, and pretending otherwise dismisses their feelings.

Results Day

Handling the Outcome Together

How you respond to a result teaches your child how to respond to life.

1

Results day carries more emotional weight than the competition itself, and your reaction in the first few minutes will be remembered long after the score is forgotten. Whatever the outcome, lead with the person, not the performance. A child who wins needs to hear that it was earned, so success feels connected to effort rather than to being inherently special — that connection is what makes them willing to work for the next one. A child who does not win needs, above all, to feel that your pride in them is completely intact.

2

Disappointment is not a problem to be solved or argued away; it is a feeling to be acknowledged. Let your child be disappointed. Sit with them in it rather than rushing to fix it. Only once the feeling has been heard is there any point in gently exploring what they learned, or looking towards the next opportunity. A parent who can say, and mean, ‘this hurts, and I’m here, and you’re going to be alright,’ gives their child something far more valuable than a win: the knowledge that they are safe to try, fail, and try again.

3

It also helps to keep perspective visible. One competition, at one moment in a child’s life, does not define their ability or their future. The students who ultimately go furthest are almost never the ones who never lost; they are the ones who learned to lose well and keep going. Every result — win or lose — is a chance to teach that lesson, and you are the one best placed to teach it.

Peace of Mind

A Platform You Can Rely On

While you focus on your child, we focus on keeping the arena safe and fair.

Safe Participation

Competitions are run responsibly, with careful handling of participant data. We collect only what we need to run the competition and issue certificates, as set out in our Privacy Policy.

Verifiable Certificates

Every certificate carries a unique ID and QR code, so any achievement your child earns can be authenticated online by anyone — a school, a scholarship body, or a future admissions officer.

Transparent Rules

Eligibility, fees where they apply, formats, and judging criteria are all published in advance. There are no hidden costs and no secret expectations.

A Level Field

Real Support

If something goes wrong — a technical issue, an unclear instruction, a genuine emergency — a real person is reachable through our support channel to help fairly.

Wellbeing First

We design competitions to encourage healthy growth, not undue pressure, and we actively discourage the kind of win-at-all-costs culture that harms young learners.

Balance

Screens, Stress & Wellbeing

Enrichment should add to a childhood, not consume it.

1

A healthy relationship with competition is a balanced one. Competitions are enrichment — a valuable addition to a full, rounded childhood — not a replacement for rest, play, friendships, and family time. If preparation is regularly eating into sleep, crowding out everything a child enjoys, or leaving them anxious for days, that is a signal to step back and recalibrate, not to push harder. A child who is exhausted and stressed will neither perform well nor grow from the experience.

2

Because our competitions are online-first, it is worth being thoughtful about screen time too. Preparation and participation may happen on a device, which is fine in itself, but it helps to keep other parts of the day screen-light, and to make sure competition does not become one more reason a child is glued to a screen from morning to night. Encourage them to plan on paper, to take real breaks, and to step outside. The mind works better for it, and so does the child.

3

Watch, gently, for the warning signs that competition has tipped from healthy to harmful: sleep disruption, loss of interest in things they used to love, unusual irritability, or a fear of the result that seems out of proportion. None of these mean competition is bad; they mean this child needs less pressure and more reassurance right now. The most successful young competitors are, almost without exception, the ones whose parents kept the whole thing in proportion.

Complement, Not Competition

Beyond the Report Card

Competitions do something school reports were never designed to do.

1

School reports measure a narrow, important set of things: whether a child has learned the syllabus and can reproduce it under exam conditions. That matters, but it is not the whole of a young person. A report card cannot easily capture originality, creativity, initiative, courage under pressure, or the ability to make something from nothing. These qualities are often the very ones that determine how far a child goes in life, and yet they rarely appear on a marksheet. Competitions exist, in part, to give these hidden strengths a place to show themselves.

2

This is why competitions should be seen as a complement to schooling, not a rival to it. A child who is average in the classroom may be extraordinary in a design brief, a debate, or an invention challenge — and discovering that can transform how they see themselves and how hard they are willing to work at everything, including school. Conversely, a strong student who has only ever been tested by exams gains something valuable from a challenge that rewards imagination rather than recall. Both children grow, in different directions, from the same experience.

3

As a parent, it helps to hold this perspective and share it with your child. A competition result is a window into one facet of their ability at one moment — not a second, competing verdict on their worth to sit alongside their report card. Framed that way, competitions relieve pressure rather than adding to it: they become a place to explore and discover, where the stakes are curiosity and growth, not grades and consequences.

Know Your Child

The Anxious One & the Over-Competitive One

Two very different children need two very different kinds of help.

1

Children bring different temperaments to competition, and the support that helps one can harm another. The anxious child wants to do well but is paralysed by the fear of failing — and often, quietly, by the fear of disappointing you. For this child, more encouragement to ‘try harder’ is exactly the wrong medicine; it adds weight to a load that is already too heavy. What helps is lowering the stakes out loud: reminding them that entering is the win, that a poor result changes nothing about how you see them, and that they are allowed to simply do their best and see what happens. Anxious children flourish when the fear of the outcome is gently removed.

2

The over-competitive child has the opposite problem. They want to win so badly that a loss feels like a catastrophe, they may struggle to celebrate others, and they can tip into unhealthy pressure entirely on their own — no push from you required. For this child, the work is teaching perspective: that effort and improvement matter more than rank, that others’ success does not diminish their own, and that how you compete — with honesty and grace — matters as much as whether you win. Celebrate their sportsmanship as loudly as their results, and be careful that your own reactions do not feed the very intensity you are trying to temper.

3

Most children are a mix of both at different moments, and part of the art of parenting a young competitor is noticing which one is in front of you today. The anxious child needs the pressure turned down; the over-competitive child needs perspective turned up. Both need the same underlying message, delivered in different tones: you are enough, exactly as you are, and this is a place to grow — not a test of your worth.

Age by Age

Support That Grows With Them

A Class 6 competitor and a Class 12 competitor need different things from you.

1

Support should evolve as your child does. For younger students, roughly in the middle-school years, competition is primarily about exposure and enjoyment. The goal is not results but a positive first taste: the thrill of trying something new, the pride of completing it, the discovery of what excites them. At this age, your role is warm and hands-on with logistics — helping them understand instructions, keeping the experience light and fun, and celebrating effort loudly. Keep the emotional stakes low, and let them fall in love with the idea of testing themselves before results ever matter.

2

For older students, approaching the senior school years, competition takes on a sharper edge of purpose. Results can genuinely matter now — a strong, verifiable achievement can support a scholarship application, a portfolio, or a university admission — and teenagers are usually capable of managing their own preparation. Here your role shifts from hands-on helper to trusted advisor and steady presence. Step back on the logistics, respect their growing independence, and focus your energy on the emotional support that even the most capable teenager still needs: perspective, reassurance, and the certainty of your unconditional pride.

3

Whatever the age, resist the urge to compare your child to where a sibling was, or where a classmate is, at the same stage. Children develop at their own pace, and a spark that shows up late is no less bright for arriving when it did. The parent who meets their child where they actually are — not where a chart says they should be — gives them the securest possible base from which to reach higher.

For You, Too

When You Feel the Pressure as Well

Parents are competitive and anxious too — and that is worth being honest about.

1

Here is something rarely said out loud: a great deal of the pressure children feel does not originate with them. It travels, quietly, from the parent. We live in a world of comparison, and it is genuinely hard to watch another child win, to hear about a classmate’s achievement, or to see your own child fall short, without feeling a pang that has nothing to do with them and everything to do with us. Recognising that pang for what it is — your feeling, not their failing — is one of the most valuable things you can do for your child.

2

Because children are extraordinarily good at reading their parents, your disappointment leaks even when your words are kind. A sigh, a slightly flat ‘well done,’ a comparison that slips out — these tell a child more than any speech. So the real work of supporting a young competitor often happens inside the parent, before a word is spoken: managing your own hopes so they do not become expectations, and separating your pride in your child from any particular result. A child cannot feel free to fail if they sense that you cannot bear it.

3

Be gentle with yourself, too. Wanting the best for your child is not a flaw; it is love. The goal is not to feel nothing, but to make sure that what your child receives from you is steadiness and warmth, whatever you happen to be feeling underneath. If you can do that — if you can be the calm in their storm rather than a second storm of your own — you will have given them something most of us spend a lifetime learning to give ourselves: the freedom to try hard at difficult things without fear.

Parent Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions we hear most often from parents and guardians.

My child is nervous about competing. Should I push them to enter?

Encourage, but don’t force. Nervousness before a challenge is normal and even healthy. Frame entering itself as the brave, worthwhile act — not winning — and make it clear the choice is theirs and your support is unconditional. A child who enters because they want to will always gain more than one who was pushed.

How involved should I be in their preparation?

Involved in the logistics, not the content. Help them protect time, plan across days, and stay rested. Be a sounding board who asks helpful questions. But the work itself must be entirely their own — both because that is what the rules require, and because that is what builds their confidence.

Is it okay to help with the actual work if my child is struggling?

No, and it matters. Completing or correcting the work yourself breaches the rules on originality that protect every honest participant, and it quietly teaches your child that they couldn’t manage alone. A genuine, imperfect effort is worth far more than a polished one that isn’t truly theirs.

What if my child doesn’t win and takes it hard?

Acknowledge the disappointment rather than dismissing it, reaffirm that your pride is unchanged, and — once the feeling is heard — gently look at what they learned and what comes next. Learning to lose well is one of the most valuable skills a competition can teach, and you are the person best placed to teach it.

Are these competitions safe for my child?

Yes. Competitions are run responsibly, participant data is handled carefully as described in our Privacy Policy, and a real support channel is available if anything goes wrong. You can read more on our Safety page.

How do I know the results are fair?

Judging is independent and merit-based, with published criteria and consistency checks. Your child competes on equal terms with every other student, regardless of school or background. Our Judging Process page explains exactly how this works.

Is there a cost, and are there hidden charges?

Requirements vary by competition. Where a fee applies, it is shown clearly before you confirm, with no hidden charges. Our Refund Policy explains how cancellations are handled.

How much time should preparation take?

Enough to prepare well, but never so much that it harms sleep, wellbeing, or the rest of childhood. If preparation is regularly crowding out everything else or causing lasting anxiety, step back — that is a signal to reduce pressure, not increase effort.

Can I enter my younger child, or do they need to be a certain age?

Each competition states its eligible grade band, typically within Grades 6 to 12. Check the specific competition page and enter your child in the correct band, which is part of what keeps the field fair for everyone.

What’s the single most important thing I can do?

Make it unmistakably clear — in words, before the result is known — that your love and pride do not depend on how they place. A child who competes from that security is free to do their best, which is also when they do their best.

Keep This Handy

The Parent’s Checklist

A quick reference for the whole journey, from entry to results day.

  • Before entering: let it be their choice, and praise the courage of trying
  • While preparing: protect time and rest; help them plan, don’t do the work
  • Ask about what interests them, not just what they expect to score
  • The night before: reassure, then insist on sleep
  • On the day: make sure they’ve eaten and are calm; then step back
  • On results day: lead with the person, not the performance
  • If they win: tie it to their effort, not to being special
  • If they don’t: acknowledge the feeling, keep your pride visible, look ahead
  • Always: keep it in proportion — one competition never defines a child
You will never sit the competition for your child. But you can be the reason they walk into it unafraid — and that is worth more than any result.For Every Parent
